Response: We thank the reviewer for this related question. We first quantified napM (Rv0047c) transcript levels across all WT conditions profiled by RNA-seq and then evaluated NapM dependence separately for hypoxia and latency (Response Fig. 4).

Under control, hypoxic and latent conditions, mean napM expression was 421, 584 and 423 CPM, respectively. Hypoxia increased napM expression 1.39-fold at the CPM level; the TMM-normalized limma estimate was log2FC = 0.93 (approximately 1.91-fold; BH-adjusted q = 2.45 × 10−4). In the latent model, expression was essentially unchanged at the CPM level (1.00-fold relative to WT), and the limma estimate indicated only a small change (log2FC = 0.15, approximately 1.11-fold; BH-adjusted q = 8.04 × 10−3). Neither change exceeded the study’s |log2FC| > 1 effect-size threshold for DEG classification. Thus, napM shows hypoxia-associated transcriptional induction but no comparable change under latency (Response Fig. 4A).

For hypoxia, we used genotype-aware analyses rather than treating pooled Hi-C map distances as measures of NapM dependence. At 5-kb resolution, 315 of 883 bins (35.7%) met the predefined detected genotype-by-hypoxia insulation-interaction classification (Response Fig. 4B). This proportion describes the operational classification and should not be interpreted as a causal fraction of hypoxia reorganization attributable to NapM.

Independent-sample analysis of 15 Hi-C samples showed that the genome-mean insulation response to hypoxia was +0.041 in WT and −0.067 in napM-KO. The genotype contrast of these responses was −0.108 (sample-level bootstrap 95% CI −0.114 to −0.102; exact oxygen-label permutation P = 4.08 × 10−4; Response Fig. 4C). This randomization test evaluates the sharp null of no oxygen-condition effect within either genotype, rather than an interaction-only null conditional on arbitrary main effects. A nine-metric structural fingerprint further demonstrated a difference in response magnitude between genotypes (Euclidean distance = 5.95, permutation P = 8.0 × 10−4), whereas response direction alone did not differ significantly (cosine similarity = −0.070, P = 0.365; Response Fig. 4D).

The baseline KO effect was also correlated with the WT hypoxia response across 881 bins (Spearman ρ = 0.527, 95% CI 0.438–0.609, circular-shift P = 0.0011; Response Fig. 4E). Under an exploratory classification, 594 of 684 hypoxia-responsive bins (86.8%) were already altered in napM-KO under control conditions. These findings support modulation of a pre-existing NapM-associated architectural state rather than a completely new structure induced only under hypoxia. They do not establish that napM deletion and hypoxia act through the same molecular pathway or quantify a unique NapM-dependent fraction of the entire hypoxia response.

For latency, five of six chromosome-organization metrics changed in the same direction as under hypoxia, generally with a larger effect in the latent state (Response Fig. 4F). However, no napM-KO-latent Hi-C sample is available because napM-KO cells lose viability during establishment of the latency model. Consequently, we cannot directly quantify how much of the latent chromosome organization depends on NapM. The viability phenotype is consistent with an important role for NapM during latency adaptation, but it neither reveals the KO-latent chromosome state nor establishes chromosome reorganization as the cause of death. Finally, the small change in napM mRNA under latency argues against a simple mechanism driven by a large alteration in napM transcript abundance, although changes in NapM protein abundance, genomic occupancy or biochemical activity remain possible.
