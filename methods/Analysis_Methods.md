# Methods for Response Figures 1–11

## Scope and notation

These methods describe the analyses underlying the eleven response figures. They supplement, rather than replace, the experimental protocols and primary data-processing methods in the manuscript. Throughout, WT denotes wild type and KO denotes the *napM* deletion strain. H37Rv genomic coordinates refer to the 4,411,532-bp circular chromosome. Unless stated otherwise, empirical P values are two-sided and are calculated as \((b+1)/(m+1)\), where \(b\) is the number of null statistics at least as extreme as the observed statistic and \(m\) is the number of valid permutations.

## Shared Hi-C, RNA-seq and NapM ChIP-seq resources

Hi-C contacts were represented as iterative-correction-balanced 5-kb matrices containing 883 genomic bins [7,8]. Genomic separation was calculated on the circular chromosome as the shorter-arc distance, \(d_{ij}=\min(|i-j|,N-|i-j|)\). Pooled matrices comprised four WT, four KO, four WT-hypoxia, three KO-hypoxia and four WT-latent biological samples; no KO-latent Hi-C data were available. Observed/expected (O/E) maps used the same-matrix contact-probability curve, P(s), as the distance-dependent expectation. Insulation was calculated from local contacts around the diagonal. For bin-level contrasts, the primary effect was the median across all between-condition replicate pairs, with the 5th–95th percentile range used as a robust descriptive interval. A detected change required this interval to lie entirely beyond ±0.25 on the log2 insulation scale. Welch tests across independent samples and circular-shift tests were used where specified; adjacent bins were not treated as independent biological replicates.

The RNA-seq resource contained two biological replicates per condition. New genotype-by-hypoxia analyses used raw counts for the eight WT, KO, WT-hypoxia and KO-hypoxia samples. Previously generated two-group limma result tables were used only where explicitly stated. The NapM (Rv0047c) ChIP-seq resource was re-curated from the supplementary data of Minch et al. [1]. It comprised 634 peaks measured in baseline WT cells and did not provide condition-specific occupancy measurements. The primary bin-level binding definition assigned a peak to every 5-kb bin intersected by its interval (454 occupied bins); assigning only the bin containing the peak centre (433 bins) was used as a sensitivity definition. Enrichment tests and graphical summaries used Python/SciPy, NumPy and pandas, with figure generation in matplotlib [16–19]. Statistical panels and final Illustrator layout are distinct steps: the repository retains the analysis scripts and the editable layout files separately.

## Response Fig. 1 | NapM occupancy, local transcription and chromatin-loop anchors

### Genome-wide occupancy and structural integration

NapM peaks, chromatin loops, insulation changes and KO-versus-WT RNA effects were mapped to common 5-kb bins. RNA effects were aggregated by the bin containing each transcription start site (TSS), so that genes sharing a TSS bin were not counted as independent structural observations. Peak-centred insulation and local-contact profiles were aligned to NapM peak positions. Local contacts were defined as the mean O/E over circular distances of 1–10 bins. Both signed and absolute effects were examined. Spatial null distributions were generated by 10,000 circular shifts of the complete genomic track, preserving autocorrelation and peak spacing. Grey envelopes in Response Fig. 1B denote the central 95% of these null distributions.

### Matched gene controls and RNA–insulation association

Each NapM-bound gene was matched to an unbound control by baseline expression and gene length using a caliper of 1.0, yielding 825 matched pairs. Cumulative distributions of absolute RNA log2 fold changes were compared using paired bootstrap confidence intervals for differences in effect magnitude and differential-expression probability. Bin-level RNA and insulation changes were compared by Spearman correlation with LOWESS used only for visualization; the final panel contains 873 bins with both measurements (Response Fig. 1D).

For predictive sensitivity analysis, complete cases were defined by finite values for the outcome and every model covariate (n = 848); neither RNA effects nor baseline expression were imputed. The baseline model M0 contained WT coverage weight, baseline insulation, baseline expression and gene count. M1 added the bin-averaged RNA log2 fold change, and M2 added binary NapM occupancy. Out-of-sample R2 was estimated using ten contiguous genomic blocks. Differences between nested models were summarized by 1,000 block bootstraps (seed 42). This was a predictive association analysis, not a mediation or causal model.

### Loop-anchor enrichment, KO recall and candidate genes

WT loops were called as described for Response Fig. 8. A loop was classified as NapM-associated at both anchors when both anchor bins intersected curated NapM peaks. Enrichment of doubly occupied loops was evaluated against 10,000 circular shifts of the ChIP-seq signal, retaining loop positions and distances. Seventeen of 41 loops were doubly occupied, compared with a null mean of 10.897 loops (1.56-fold; empirical P = 0.0328). Independent KO loop calls were generated with Chromosight using the same detection settings. WT-loop recovery required correspondence at both anchors within ±1 bin; 100 candidate KO calls were considered. Recovery rates of 6/17 doubly occupied and 14/24 other WT loops are descriptive because of the small counts and detection differences between maps. For Response Fig. 1G, non-recovery was calculated as 1 − recovery, giving 11/17 (64.7%) doubly occupied loops and 10/24 (41.7%) other loops; non-recovery is not proof of physical loop disappearance.

For locus prioritization, 11 loops with \(|\Delta\log_2(\mathrm{loop\ O/E})|\geq0.5\) were selected, yielding 22 anchor instances at 21 unique genomic positions. RNA-tested genes with TSSs within ±10 kb of these anchors (n = 406) were intersected with the 820 baseline KO-responsive DEGs, yielding 79 candidates. For enrichment, the 79 genes formed a single query set and the 4,060 RNA-tested genes formed the primary background. KEGG and GO terms were tested by one-sided hypergeometric tests; Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied across all tested terms in each database, including terms with zero query overlap [6]. No term passed the global FDR threshold in the primary analysis, so nominal P values are displayed as exploratory annotation. Backgrounds restricted to 1,118 eligible-anchor RNA-tested genes and 226 eligible-anchor DEGs were examined separately as sensitivity analyses.

## Response Fig. 2 | Genotype-by-hypoxia transcriptional and structural responses

### Factorial RNA-seq model

Validated gene identifiers were mapped to raw counts for 3,814 genes with measurements in all four genotype–condition groups. Library sizes were normalized by edgeR TMM, mean–variance weights were estimated with limma-voom, and a four-group factor model was fitted using limma [2–5]. The contrast of interest was

\[
(KO_{hypoxia}-KO_{normoxia})-(WT_{hypoxia}-WT_{normoxia}).
\]

Empirical-Bayes moderation used `eBayes(robust = FALSE)`. P values were adjusted across the 3,814 genes using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Interaction DEGs required FDR < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1, yielding 730 genes (212 positive and 518 negative interactions). The interaction uses RNA-seq alone; Hi-C effects enter only in the subsequent bin-level integration.

For Response Fig. 2C, hypoxia-associated insulation changes were calculated per 5-kb bin as hypoxia minus normoxia for WT and ΔnapM. Violin outlines, bin-level points, means and medians describe the resulting distributions. Independent-sample genome-mean responses were also calculated from 15 Hi-C samples (WT and WT-hypoxia, n = 4 each; napM-KO, n = 4; napM-KO-hypoxia, n = 3), yielding +0.041 in WT and −0.067 in ΔnapM. The genotype contrast was −0.108 (exact oxygen-label permutation P = 4.08 × 10⁻⁴). The bin-level distributions and independent-sample means are displayed together but are not treated as equivalent estimators.

### Joint RNA–insulation effects and operational evidence classes

Gene-level RNA effects were summarized by TSS bin. Insulation interactions used the same difference-in-differences contrast as the RNA model. Genome-wide RNA–insulation coupling was assessed using Spearman correlation and 10,000 circular shifts (seed 20261030); 20-bin moving-block bootstrap intervals were used as spatial sensitivity summaries. The joint scatterplot describes associations rather than assigning causality.

Four mutually exclusive response modes were defined without using NapM binding. RNA-associated-only bins had a local RNA interaction DEG but no detected genotype × hypoxia insulation interaction (292 bins). Structure-associated-only bins had a detected insulation interaction but no local RNA interaction DEG (144 bins). Joint-response bins had both a local RNA interaction DEG and a detected insulation interaction (171 bins). The remaining 276 bins were labelled other/unresolved. Thus, RNA-associated-only bins represented 48.1% of the 607 responsive bins, and joint responses represented 54.3% of the 315 structural-interaction bins. NapM-binding enrichment was tested post hoc for each response mode against all other bins using two-sided Fisher exact tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction; no class showed enrichment (BH q = 0.943). Response-mode frequencies describe how common each pattern is and do not establish pathway importance, direction of causality or NapM-independent mechanisms. Lack of a detected local DEG does not exclude distal or indirect transcriptional effects.

For locus plots, hypoxia/normoxia O/E difference maps, four-condition insulation profiles and gene annotations were aligned to genomic coordinates. Contact values are displayed at native 5-kb resolution using non-interpolated `pcolormesh` cells; no bilinear interpolation was used. Representative loci were selected by effect size, local RNA-testing completeness and interpretability: dinG illustrates RNA-associated-only responses with minimal focal insulation change, recR illustrates stringent structure-associated responses, and accA2/accD2/fadE13 illustrates a concordant joint response. Focal-gene RNA interaction log2FC values are shown as TSS-aligned lollipops with dashed lines at ±1 to make the DEG effect-size threshold explicit. These examples describe the response modes and do not establish causal relationships or transcriptional synergy. The working-model panel summarizes these observed classes and is not an additional statistical test.

## Response Fig. 3 | GO and KEGG enrichment of baseline KO-responsive genes

Baseline KO-versus-WT DEGs were stratified into 460 upregulated and 362 downregulated genes (822 total). KEGG annotations were obtained through the KEGG REST API for organism `mtu`; GO annotations were obtained from the EBI GOA proteome file for *M. tuberculosis* ATCC 25618, with term labels and ontology namespaces from `go-basic.obo`. Downloaded annotation resources were cached for reproducibility. Enrichment was tested separately for up- and downregulated genes by one-sided hypergeometric tests against detected genes annotated in each database (1,122 for KEGG; 3,111 for GO). Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied within the corresponding annotation database and direction-specific analysis, with q < 0.05 considered significant [6]. KEGG displays also include nominal-P rankings where indicated in the figure.

## Response Fig. 4 | NapM expression and stress-associated genome reorganization

NapM transcript abundance was calculated from raw counts as counts per million (CPM) after library-size normalization; individual RNA-seq replicate values are shown. Separate two-group TMM-limma comparisons supplied hypoxia/WT and latent/WT effect estimates [3,5]. Genome-organization distances were calculated as \(1-r\), where \(r\) is the Pearson correlation between normalized genome-wide contact values of pooled 5-kb matrices, excluding the first five diagonals.

Long-range contact fractions were calculated for each bin from pooled matrices by summing contacts outside the central ten-bin separation range and dividing by the bin's total contacts. Hypoxia responses were the WT-hypoxia minus WT and KO-hypoxia minus KO bin-wise differences; the WT-latent response was included for reference. The bin-wise Mann–Whitney comparison shown in Response Fig. 4C describes the spatial distribution of pooled-matrix effects and is not a test across biological replicates. NapM-dependence was evaluated separately using all 15 independent WT, KO, WT-hypoxia and KO-hypoxia samples. Genome-mean insulation differences were tested by exact permutation of condition labels, with sample-level bootstrap confidence intervals, and a nine-metric response fingerprint was compared using Euclidean-distance and cosine-similarity statistics. These independent-sample analyses, rather than the pooled per-bin P value, support the genotype-dependent response magnitude. The absence of KO-latent data precludes a corresponding latent interaction estimate.

## Response Fig. 5 | NapM binding, sequence context and interaction evidence

The 634 curated NapM peaks [1] were merged to calculate genome coverage, and coverage was recalculated across peak-score thresholds for sensitivity analysis. Continuous ChIP-seq signal was summarized independently from the bedGraph track. Spatial clustering was assessed using the mean nearest-neighbour distance of peak centres with circular distances and 10,000 random placements of 634 points (seed 42).

Peak GC content was compared with the genome and with 1,000 length-matched random interval sets. Enriched 8-mers were evaluated against length- and GC-matched backgrounds. Peaks were assigned to mutually exclusive gene-body, promoter (TSS −300 bp) and intergenic categories; positional enrichment was compared with 1,000 circular shifts that preserved genomic autocorrelation.

For binding–expression integration, genes were assigned to promoter-bound, gene-body-bound or operon-extended tiers. Operon extension was explicitly treated as approximate because operon metadata cover only a subset of genes. DEG frequencies across binding tiers were compared by Fisher's exact tests. The association between maximum per-gene peak score and |RNA log2 fold change| was assessed by Spearman correlation. The integrated network comprised 427 NapM DNA–protein associations and 14 protein–protein associations from the author-built PPI resource. PPI neighbours were annotated against MycoBrowser. The network contained no NapM self-edge; missing edges were not interpreted as evidence that an interaction cannot occur.

## Response Fig. 6 | Circular eigenvectors and saddle analysis

For each pooled matrix, contacts were divided by the mean contact frequency at the corresponding circular distance. Pearson correlations between bin-wise O/E interaction profiles were eigendecomposed, and the leading eigenvector was examined without interpreting it as a eukaryotic A/B compartment label. Eigenvector signs were fixed only for plotting; harmonic associations are reported as absolute correlations.

For the Response Fig. 6 control analysis, bins between the 2nd and 98th percentiles of PC1 were divided into 20 equal-occupancy quantile groups. After excluding the first two diagonals, the mean O/E was calculated for every group pair. Segregation strength was defined from the outer 20% corner blocks as \(\log_2[\mu(AA\cup BB)/\mu(AB)]\), following saddle aggregation principles [14]. Uncertainty was estimated by leave-one-group-out jackknifing, and between-condition comparisons used jackknife z-tests. Saddle-pattern similarity to WT was measured by Pearson correlation across saddle cells, with 100 label permutations and the mean pairwise similarity of four WT replicates as references.

Two controls distinguished genuine structure from methodological artefacts. First, a synthetic matrix was generated solely from WT P(s), scaled to the observed total contact count, Poisson sampled, and passed through the same pipeline. Second, PC1 labels were permuted 100 times while all other parameters were held fixed. PC1 was compared with \(\cos(2\pi x/L)\), distance from oriC, GC content and gene density. To remove the primary ori–ter axial predictor, O/E at each circular distance was regressed on \(|\cos\theta_i-\cos\theta_j|\), where \(\theta=2\pi x/L\). The residual matrix was reanalysed by eigendecomposition and saddle aggregation. Because residuals can be negative, the residual corner contrast was expressed as \(\mu(AA\cup BB)-\mu(AB)\), not as a log ratio. Residual PC1 was compared with \(\cos(4\pi x/L)\); segment boundaries were obtained from zero crossings after circular Savitzky–Golay smoothing.

## Response Fig. 7 | Directionality index and CID validation

The directionality index (DI) was calculated on the balanced 5-kb matrix, not a 100-kb matrix, following the directional-contact framework of Le et al. [10,11]. The matrix was Gaussian-smoothed (σ = 2 bins), log1p-transformed and converted to a bin-wise Pearson correlation matrix. For each bin, upstream and downstream contacts within 100 kb (20 bins per side) were compared by a paired two-sided t-test; the t statistic was used as DI.

Candidate CID boundaries required DI ≤ −2 within ten upstream bins and DI ≥ +2 within ten downstream bins. The bin with the largest positive DI jump was selected at each transition, and adjacent domains were required to span at least 100 kb. DI was independently calculated for four WT biological replicates. Confirmation required a transition within ±25 kb in at least three replicates, yielding 13 confirmed boundaries and five lower-confidence candidates.

Local boundary pileups were generated with coolpup.py using local O/E normalization and ±150-kb flanks [9]. Insulation crosses were summarized as arms-to-corners ratios in the merged matrix and each replicate. Boundary-aligned DI and insulation metaplots were compared with distance-matched random positions. Within-CID and cross-boundary contacts at 30–150 kb were compared using 1,000 random partitions preserving the CID size distribution (seed 42). Response Fig. 7 ends at panel G; whole-CID rescaled pileups from an earlier exploratory version are not part of the final figure or its evidence set.

## Response Fig. 8 | Chromosight loop calling and validation

Loops were called on the balanced 5-kb WT matrix using Chromosight v1.6.3 with the loop kernel, minimum template Pearson correlation 0.3 and anchor distances of 30 kb–1 Mb [12]. All 41 retained WT calls had Chromosight q < 0.05. Because internal q values can be affected by pixel dependence, independent empirical controls were also examined.

For aggregate peak analysis (APA), loop positions were centred on the midpoint between anchors, and ±50-kb O/E windows were averaged. Focal enrichment was summarized by the central 25-kb square. The same procedure was applied to distance-matched random locus pairs, +100-kb shifted positions, each of four WT replicates and the KO map. An empirical FDR curve was calculated as the fraction of shifted-null positions exceeding each anchor-pixel O/E threshold divided by the corresponding fraction of called loops. Loop-versus-null anchor O/E values were compared with a Mann–Whitney test; paired WT-versus-KO values were compared with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. These analyses test focal enrichment and reproducibility of the called set rather than physical loop bridging by NapM.

## Response Fig. 9 | Quantitative WT-versus-KO compaction metrics

All experimental metrics were calculated independently for each of four WT and four KO ICE-balanced matrices using circular distances and within-replicate normalization. Contact fractions corresponded to <100 kb, 100 kb–1 Mb and >1 Mb. Compaction slope was the log–log slope of P(s) over 100 kb–1 Mb, and median contact distance was the separation at which the cumulative contact-distance distribution reached 0.5.

Mirror-arm O/E was averaged over locus pairs \((x,L-x)\) within a ±10-kb band and with circular distance >500 kb, using the same-replicate P(s) as expectation; profiles were also summarized in 20 arm windows. Ori–ter O/E used ori at 0 ± 50 kb and the GC-skew-defined ter region at 2.03 Mb ± 50 kb. Raw, depth-normalized and distance-normalized secondary-diagonal signals were reported separately. WT–KO comparisons used two-sided Welch t-tests and Mann–Whitney tests; with n = 4 per group, 0.029 is the smallest possible exact two-sided Mann–Whitney P value.

Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated with LorDG at 5-kb resolution (883 beads) [13]. Radius of gyration, all-bead pairwise distances, ori–ter distances and mirror-arm distances used locus definitions analogous to the Hi-C metrics. Only one reconstruction was available per condition; model-derived quantities are therefore descriptive and were not subjected to significance tests. The ori–ter map–model discrepancy is retained rather than reconciled by additional parameter selection.

## Response Fig. 10 | Experimental evaluation of alternative ori–ter mechanisms

Ori–ter O/E, mirror-arm O/E, contact fractions and P(s) slopes were computed per biological replicate as described for Response Fig. 9. WT, hypoxia and latent conditions each contained four biological replicates; group comparisons used two-sided Welch t-tests. Global condensation, local ori–ter tethering and ring/toroid refolding were compared by their joint predictions across these metrics, rather than by ori–ter O/E alone.

A FISH feasibility pilot used a single probe targeted to the ter region (2.03 Mb), with DAPI counterstaining, in four replicate fixed-cell samples. Overview fields and magnified insets document probe detectability. Because only the ter locus was labelled and cell clumping prevented reliable single-cell segmentation, these images were not used to calculate ori–ter distances or to infer three-dimensional compaction. Dual ori/ter labelling and improved declumping would be required for that analysis.

## Response Fig. 11 | OpenMM ring-polymer scenario comparison

The circular chromosome was represented by 441 coarse-grained beads (10 kb per bead), with bead 0 at ori and bead 203 at ter. Simulations used OpenMM 8.1.1 at 300 K [15]. Lengths were expressed in bead-diameter units (σ ≈ 50 nm). The force field included ring-closed harmonic bonds (rest length 1σ, k = 100 kT/σ2), harmonic angle bending where specified, WCA excluded volume (ε = 1 kT), and a continuous Gaussian non-specific attraction for S1 and S3. Harmonic-wall cylindrical confinement had a 6:1 aspect ratio (R = 5σs; half-length = 30σs), where s is the confinement scale. Dynamics used the LangevinMiddle integrator, friction coefficient 1 ps−1 and timestep 0.01 ps after a warm-up ramp.

Chains were initialized as a 20-turn helix and energy-minimized. A temporary soft restraint was used during initial rephasing to place ori and ter near opposite cylinder poles, followed by unrestrained equilibration. Production comprised 2.1 × 10^6 steps with 700 conformations saved every 3,000 steps. Energy, Rg and ori–ter distance plateaus, block-wise drift and Rg autocorrelation were examined. Effective sample sizes were estimated from autocorrelation and were substantially smaller than 700 for some scenarios; independent-seed runs and initial-condition controls were retained.

The baseline confinement scale was selected from {0.7, 0.85, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4} by mean-centred log-space P(s) RMSE against WT over 10 kb–2.2 Mb; s = 0.7 was selected. A contact cutoff of 3.8σ was calibrated once on the baseline to reproduce the WT short-range fraction and then held fixed. Four scenarios were examined: S1, global attraction; S2, a harmonic ori–ter tether between beads 0 and 203; S3, coupled bending stiffness and weak attraction; and S3+apposition, S3 plus a local ori–ter tether. Scenario strengths were selected by P(s)-shape RMSE against the latent experiment. Ori–ter O/E and arm–arm O/E were never used in fitting, calibration or model selection and served only as held-out checks.

Simulated metrics used the same circular-distance and P(s)-normalization definitions as the experimental analysis. Models were compared using regularized Gaussian pseudo-likelihood AIC/BIC with a 5%-of-mean variance floor; unregularized values were also retained. No scenario reproduced both held-out metrics quantitatively. The S3 optimum was bistable across seeds and had a small effective sample size, and collapsed S1 states showed seed-dependent internal arrangements. Consequently, the simulations provide directional scenario discrimination, not unique three-dimensional structures, high-precision parameter estimates or evidence for a specific molecular tether.

## Reproducibility boundaries

All analysis scripts, selected input resources, derived tables and final figures are indexed in the repository manifests. Original scripts are preserved for auditability, including original absolute project paths; repository-relative launchers and a path map identify the configuration needed for rerunning. Final Illustrator files preserve editable text and represent figure assembly, not statistical recomputation. Archived exploratory figures and superseded response drafts are excluded. The package does not imply that KO-latent measurements, condition-specific NapM ChIP-seq, dual-probe FISH distances, replicate LorDG ensembles or new OpenMM simulations were generated during preparation of this repository.
